The short version

Daysheet collects no personal data. There is no account, no sign-up and no analytics. The app makes no network requests of any kind. The children in your care, their dates of birth, their allergies, their photographs and everything you log about their day stay in Daysheet's own storage on your iPhone, and nobody — including the developer — can see them.

What Daysheet collects

Nothing. In App Store Connect terms, the app's data collection is declared as Data Not Collected, and that declaration is accurate rather than aspirational: the app contains no analytics framework, no advertising identifier, no crash reporter and no third-party SDK of any kind. It does not read your contacts, your location or your calendar, and it does not ask for permission to.

Daysheet asks for two permissions and only when you use the features that need them: notifications, if you turn on the end-of-day sheet reminder, and access to a photo you pick when you attach one to a moment. Declining either leaves the rest of the app working normally.

What stays on your device

Everything you enter: children's names and dates of birth, allergy, dietary and medical flags, parents' names, contracted hours and rates, every meal, nap, diaper or nappy change, moment and mood you log, attendance times, incident records, medication logs, and your own settings. It is written to a single file inside the app's container, which iOS keeps private to Daysheet, plus a shared App Group container holding a short summary so the widgets can display it.

This data is included in an encrypted iPhone or iCloud backup if you make one. That backup is between you and Apple; the developer has no access to it.

Children's data, and whose it is

Almost everything in Daysheet is personal data about somebody else's child, and in several jurisdictions data about a child's health — an allergy, a medication, an injury — is a special category of personal data requiring particular care. Daysheet is built on the assumption that it must never leave your device, which is why the app has no account system, no cloud sync, no backup service of its own and no network capability at all: not as a setting you could switch on, but as a property of the software.

You, not the developer, are responsible for this data. Under the GDPR and comparable laws you are the data controller for the records you keep about the children in your care; the developer receives none of it and is not a processor of it. Your own obligations to those families — telling them what you record, keeping it accurate, keeping it secure, and not keeping it longer than you need to — are unchanged by using this app. Daysheet is a filing cabinet, not a legal position.

It follows that the sensible thing is to tell parents you keep a daily record on your phone, what is in it, and how long you keep it. Most already assume you do.

Photographs

A photograph of a child is personal data and, in most settings, is covered by a permission the parent gave you. Daysheet stores photos as ordinary image files inside its own container, never uploads them, and never writes them to your camera roll. The only way one leaves the device is on a sheet you share yourself. Deleting the entry deletes the photo with it.

The sheets you send

When you send a sheet, Daysheet builds the PDF on your iPhone and hands it to the iOS share sheet. What happens next is between you, the app you chose and the parent — the developer is not involved and cannot see it. Choose that channel with the same care you would apply to a paper record, since a sheet contains a named child, their day and sometimes their health.

What Apple sees

Purchases. If you subscribe to Full House, Apple processes the payment. The app receives only a signed receipt saying whether a subscription is active — never your name, your email or your payment details. Apple's handling of that transaction is covered by Apple's privacy policy.

App Store analytics. Apple reports aggregate, anonymised figures to developers, such as how many devices installed the app in a country. These cannot identify you or any child, and the app plays no part in producing them.

Your rights and the parents'

Data protection law — including the UK and EU GDPR, and comparable laws elsewhere — gives people rights of access, correction, erasure, portability and objection over personal data held about them, and a parent may exercise those rights on behalf of their child.

Those requests come to you, because you hold the records. Daysheet gives you what you need to answer them: every entry can be viewed and edited in the app, a child's sheets can be exported as PDFs, attendance can be exported as a CSV, and a child's whole record can be deleted permanently. Because the developer holds nothing, there is nothing here to disclose, correct or delete on anyone's behalf.

Keeping and deleting records

Your register is kept for as long as you keep the app installed, and no longer. Archiving a child takes them off the register and keeps their records, which is usually what you want — licensing authorities commonly require records to be retained for a period after a child leaves, and that period is set by them, not by this app. Deleting a child removes their sheets, attendance, incident records, medication logs and photos permanently, with no way to recover them.

Deleting the app removes its container and everything in it. There is no server-side copy to request the deletion of, and no way to get any of it back — so export what you are required to keep before you do.

Security

Your data is protected by iOS's own file protection, which encrypts app data at rest while the device is locked. Setting a passcode, and turning on Face ID or Touch ID, is what makes that protection meaningful; an unlocked phone with no passcode protects nothing. Given that this app holds other people's children's health information, that is worth doing properly.

Because no data is transmitted, there is no network channel to intercept and no server to breach.

Changes

If this policy changes, the date at the top changes with it, and the previous wording stays in the public history of the repository that hosts this page. A change that materially affected how data is handled would be announced in the app before it took effect.
